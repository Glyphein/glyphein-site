Glyphein Flow Globe

An origin-destination map for Power BI, on a 3D globe or a flat map. You give it three columns — where from, where to, how much — and it draws the network.

  • Place names, not coordinates

    More than 32,000 cities, ports and countries are geocoded inside the visual. You drop the names your data already contains; there is no latitude column to build.

  • No network calls

    No map tiles, no geocoding service, no telemetry, no fonts fetched from a CDN. The visual declares no privileges and reaches nothing outside the report. It works in tenants that block external services, and it keeps working offline.

  • From continents to cities

    Zoomed out, the map shows continent to continent. Zoom in and it resolves to countries, then to cities and ports — so two thousand routes read as a network rather than a tangle.

  • Against plan, against last year

    Add a target and the map shows which corridors run under plan. Add last year's value and it shows what appeared, what went, what grew, what fell.

The name

Glyphein — γλύφειν, “to engrave, to carve”; said gli-fine. Where γράφειν lays ink on a surface, γλύφειν takes material away.

The sea charts and portolans of the 16th to 19th centuries were all cut with a burin on copper — a direct lineage, and we make maps. In the lineage of Bertin, a glyph is the graphic mark that encodes a value: the name describes what the product makes.
